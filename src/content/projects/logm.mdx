## In the Beginning...

In January 1989 I graduated from "The Honeywell Bull Institute for Computer Programming" in Calgary. This was a six-month course covering COBOL, Fortran, C, JCL, systems analysis, and job-hunting and interviewing skills. I took this so I would have something to show when applying for jobs other than "Played with a Color Computer in high school".

My first programming job was with GMA (Geophysical Microcomputer Applications) in Calgary, starting not long after I graduated. I responded to this newspaper ad (yes, I'm THAT old):

## Day One

On my first day I was given a Compaq portable, a floppy disk with some C source code, and a vague description of a bug to hunt down. This was soon replaced by an actual desktop computer, but the Compaq always sticks in my mind.

This was my first time encountering non-toy C code, and I was quite overwhelmed. My imposter syndrome was in full force, and the next morning I called in and told them they had made a horrible mistake in hiring me, and I tried to quit.

Fortunately, my manager, Dennis, wasn't having it, and he convinced me to come back in. I settled down and began several years of late nights and weekends, absorbing everything I could about C, assembly language, dBASE, and eventually Unix and networking.

For dBASE, we used a library from Sequiter Software in Edmonton, named CodeBase. I was curious, and it turns out they remained in business much longer than I would have expected (~2015), finally releasing the libraries as open source in 2018: https://github.com/MPSystemsServices/CodeBase-for-DBF.

I didn't have much of a life outside of work (other than going to a lot of movies, and playing Arkanoid at an arcade a few blocks from my apartment), but I don't regret it; it was very educational and satisfying. I've since been told by Dennis that I tried to quit one or two more times after that. I have no memory of that, but it fits.

The actual software was, as I described it, "Software for making squiggles". I didn't know the difference between a Fast Fourier Transform and a Quarter Pounder with Cheese, but it was rarely a problem. I was building user interfaces for DOS, and later graphical interfaces using a graphics library (still DOS), and when math was needed, it was supplied and I plugged it in.

That program I was given to troubleshoot on day one was for printing said squiggles to a dot matrix printer, and the bug manifested when printing very long output (many, many pages). I don't know that I ever solved it, nor did anyone else in my memory. I eventually rewrote it to work with a DOS extender that ran in protected mode so it could use 32-bit offsets, and the bug survived that! I was sure it was an over- or underflow issue, and I used the tools we had at the time (e.g. [PC-Lint](https://www.vector.com/en/product/pc-lint-plus/), [BoundsChecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BoundsChecker)), but never quite found it. I'm sure an LLM would have solved it in 0.3 seconds.

## LogM

I mostly worked on a software package named LogM. It made squiggles. Unfortunately screenshots of DOS software from the early 1990s are hard to come by, so I don't have anything but vague memories of the menu system that was used to launch the various modules.

One of the modules was named "sos" and was invoked by the menu system to exit the menu system (via returning a particular exit code). I was quite proud to convert that from a regular DOS `.exe` to a `.com` that took a whopping 5 bytes on disk. It loaded the return code into a register and invoked the DOS terminate interrupt. Nobody cared but me, but I thought it was super-great.

## Still Kicking

Long after my tenure at GMA, they sold the rights to LogM (by then a Windows application) to another company (Landmark/Halliburton, yes, that Halliburton), and it is still being sold as part of GVERSE GeoGraphix (at version 2026.2 as I write this). I doubt any code remains from my era, but this makes it an older product than Microsoft Office, in terms of things I've worked on.
